Slow down a backing track without changing the pitch

macOS 14+ · free beta

If slowing a track down also makes it sound flat, the player is changing the sample rate instead of time-stretching. You need a tool that separates speed from pitch. On a Mac, JamCrate slows audio from 1.0× down to 0.5× with the pitch preserved, and keeps your markers and tab on screen while it does — but it deliberately does not go above the original speed.

Why slowing down normally goes flat

Digital audio is a stream of samples at a fixed rate — 44100 or 48000 per second. The cheap way to play it slower is to push those samples out at a lower rate: 22050 instead of 44100. Everything stretches, including the frequencies, so the whole track lands an octave lower. That is exactly what a turntable does when you drop the speed, and it is why the old trick of learning a solo at half speed meant learning it in the wrong key.

What time-stretching does instead

Time-stretching analyses the audio in short overlapping windows, works out where the pitched content is, and rebuilds it over a longer duration while leaving the frequencies alone. The result is a track at 70% speed that is still in E. It is not free: the more you stretch, the more the algorithm has to invent, and dense distorted mixes start to sound smeared. Practically, 0.5× is usable, 0.3× is mush, and acoustic material survives better than a wall of guitars.

How to actually use it

  1. Find your clean tempo. Start slower than you think you need. If you make a mistake, you are still too fast.
  2. Loop the hard bars, not the whole solo. Four bars on repeat for five minutes beats the whole solo once. See looping a solo.
  3. Climb in small steps. When it is clean three times in a row, go up about ten percent. Going from 60% straight to 100% teaches you nothing except where you fail.
  4. Finish at full speed. The point of slowing down is to be able to leave it. End every session with a couple of passes at 1.0×.

While you are at it: why some tracks are louder than others

The other half of practising with a folder of tracks is volume. A mastered commercial backing track and a home-made jam track can sit twelve decibels apart, so every song change is a reach for the volume knob. JamCrate measures each file once when it is imported and stores a gain correction, so tracks arrive at roughly the same perceived level. The correction is limited to about ±12 dB, which means a badly recorded file will not be blasted into distortion just to hit a target.

Questions

Does slowing down damage my audio file?

No. Time-stretching happens during playback; the file on disk is never rewritten. Set the tempo back to 1.0× and the track is untouched.

Can I practise with a slowed track on my phone?

Yes — the set can open on a phone in the same Wi-Fi and the audio streams from the Mac, with the transport and the solo loops on the phone screen.

Why only down to 0.5×?

Below half speed the artefacts get loud enough that you start learning the smearing instead of the notes. If you genuinely need 0.3×, a DAW will do it better.
